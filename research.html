Research

I study how authoritarian power travels through political hierarchies and institutions, not only through rulers and their inner circles. Across linked projects on personalist power and authoritarian institutions, I develop broader comparative arguments about political selection, state capacity, and political legitimacy, with China serving as the primary empirical setting in which I test them.

My dissertation, Personalist Power Beyond the Inner Circle, treats personalization as an organizational process. Its three chapters examine how personalist rule is reproduced below the national leadership through delegated political selection, adaptive loyalty signaling, and leader-centered narrative construction.

Job Market Paper

Selecting for Restraint: Personalist Power and Delegated Political Selection

Yuge Wang · Under Review

I develop a theory of delegated political selection under personalism: because rulers’ trusted agents gain little independent political credit from subordinates’ successes but remain exposed to blame for their failures, they select for restrained, controllable, and defensible governing styles.

Peer-Reviewed Publication

Authoritarian Elasticity: How Autocracies May Effectively Mobilize for Crisis Management

Yuge Wang and Rongbin Han · Governance 38(4), e70074 (2025)

We develop authoritarian elasticity to explain how high-capacity autocracies may sustain crisis mobilization by repurposing existing institutions, recalibrating their functions and intensity as conditions change, and scaling mobilization back when circumstances permit.

Manuscript in Revision

Trapped: Chinese Overseas Youth’s Structured Pragmatism Amid US–China Rivalry

Wendy Weile Zhou, Yuge Wang, and Rongbin Han · Revise and resubmit

We develop structured pragmatism to explain how Chinese students and young professionals in the United States navigate geopolitical and immigration uncertainty by investing in portable resources and preserving future options rather than committing to a fixed destination.

Working Papers

Adapting to Personalism: How Local Elites Respond to Power Personalization in China

Yuge Wang

I explain how local elites adapt when authoritarian power becomes personalized, arguing that heightened uncertainty makes visible loyalty a strategy for political security and advancement, especially among officials who lack indirect ties through the ruler’s protégés.

Centralizing the Narrative: How Protégés Construct Regime Personalization

Yuge Wang

I explain how leader-centered narratives are constructed below the national leadership, arguing that dependence and competition encourage protégés to signal loyalty through local propaganda and agenda-building and that these dispersed efforts can aggregate into a centralized political narrative.

Microfoundations of Preemptive State Capacity: Evidence from China’s Grid Management System

Yuge Wang and Gary Ziwen Zu

We theorize local legibility as a form of preemptive state capacity: by turning neighborhoods into information units and embedding observation in routine service contact, grassroots institutions can help the state identify and address disorder before it escalates.

Projects in Progress

From One Exam to Many: The Scope of Commensuration and Perceptions of Social Fairness in China

Yuge Wang

I argue that meritocratic institutions shape perceived social fairness not only through allocation rules but also through the scope of commensuration—how broadly a common standard makes people comparable—even when merit-based ranking remains intact within local arenas.

Rights Without Innocence: Self-Implication and Procedural Rights-Claiming under Authoritarian Rule by Law

Yuge Wang

I theorize that self-implication—recognizing that a legal prohibition may encompass one’s own past conduct—can reduce willingness to claim procedural rights in unrelated encounters with public authorities, even without admitting illegality or expecting detection or punishment.

Born into Distrust: Targeted Repression and the Intergenerational Trust Deficit in Local Governance

Yuge Wang

I examine whether targeted political repression leaves an intergenerational trust deficit, shaping how descendants understand and evaluate local government long after the original episode of repression.
